In memory

Lester & Winnie Bradford

A Maine farm boy who became a forester, and an Oklahoma girl who became a doctor. They met at Yale, married in a mission church in Sierra Leone, and spent the rest of their long lives putting their skills to work wherever they were needed.

Dr. Winifred Smith Bradford 1922–2020 Lester Ezra Bradford 1926–2019
Their story

She delivered thousands of babies — in a mission clinic, a mud-walled village, a converted house in Washington State and a birthing center in war-torn Juba. He taught oxen to answer to gee and haw in Sierra Leone, raised tilapia and teak, and grew a million tree seedlings a year in Pakistan. For more than half a century the work was the same wherever they went: deliver the baby, plant the tree, and teach someone else to do it.

i

Two beginnings

Oklahoma · Nebraska · Kentucky / Maine · Montana · Oregon — 1922 to 1949

Winifred Ruth Smith

A doctor, against the odds of her era

Winifred Smith was born in Enid, Oklahoma, on October 20, 1922, the sixth of eight children — two years after American women won the vote. She set her heart on medicine and paid her own way through the Depression and the war: a degree from York College, a small United Brethren school in York, Nebraska, in 1944, and then the University of Nebraska College of Medicine, where she was one of four women in a class of 92.16 Her medical degree was conferred on April 3, 1948.1

She spent medical-school summers volunteering at the Red Bird Mission in the mountains of eastern Kentucky, and later went back to cover for its doctor. Not every patient was ready for her. “If I’d o knowed you was a woman,” one told her, “I wouldn’t have come.”1

Lester Ezra Bradford

Farm boy, air cadet, forester, smokejumper

Lester was born on January 20, 1926, on a farm on Mt. Abram near the village of Locke Mills, Maine, one of nine children of Everett and Fannie Bradford. “I grew up on a Maine farm where we farmed with horses and oxen,” he wrote — a fact that would matter a great deal in West Africa.32

After Hebron Academy he joined the Army in 1944 and trained as a tail gunner; just as he was ready for service, the war ended.37 The GI Bill took him to Yale for three years of botany and two in the School of Forestry, finishing with a Master of Forestry in 1951.28

He spent four summers with the U.S. Forest Service, two of them as a smokejumper parachuting onto wildfires out of Missoula, Montana, and Cave Junction, Oregon.23

ii

Head over heels, at Yale

New Haven · Kentucky · London — 1949 to 1952

Winnie’s mission board meant to send her to China, and enrolled her at the Yale Institute of Far Eastern Languages to learn Mandarin. While she was there, the communist movement swept the country and Christian missionaries were no longer permitted. China was closed; the vocation was not.1

At the Methodist Wesley Foundation in New Haven she met a forestry student. The one meeting she remembered clearly was the night she demonstrated a tumbling move learned at York College — and tossed him over her head. He landed on something sharp, and carried the scar for life. They agreed to keep writing.1

She was reassigned to Sierra Leone. After studying at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine — and singing in the Festival of Britain choir at the Royal Albert Hall — she sailed from Liverpool on the M.V. Accra on August 2, 1951, and landed in Freetown nine days later.1 Lester, meanwhile, studied tropical soils and horticulture at Cornell and had, in the Sherbro Foundation’s words, “to be satisfied with letters.”26

Winnie had arranged everything herself — the menu, the rice, the invitations, even the license, which the District Commissioner had to amend because it was unusual for the bride to apply. They stayed married for sixty-six years.16 Some later accounts give Lester’s arrival as December 20; Winnie’s letter from that month says the 21st.

iii

Seventeen years in Sierra Leone

Rotifunk · Kayima · Manjama — 1951 to 1968

Winnie began at the Evangelical United Brethren mission hospital in Rotifunk, in Bumpeh Chiefdom — a mission whose hospital is named for Marietta Hatfield and Mary Archer, two of the missionaries killed there in the Hut Tax War of 1898.9 For her first months she was the only doctor for a large area, with more than two hundred people gathering for clinic each day. One night, feeling unable to help a sick child, she cried, “I can’t do this anymore.” Her colleagues consoled her, and she carried on — and started a leprosy clinic that grew to nearly three hundred registered patients.1

After the wedding the couple chose a remote posting among the Kono people: Kayima, in eastern Sierra Leone. They lived in a thatch-roofed, mud-walled house, learned the Kono language, and wondered — in Lester’s words — “how to contribute to the welfare of our fellow villagers.”21 While Winnie helped local women deliver babies in sanitary conditions, Lester bought a pair of oxen and trained them to the plow, dug wells, bred poultry and started an adult literacy class — the projects described below.2

In September 1956 they moved to Manjama, four miles from Bo, where Lester ran the mission’s demonstration farm for a dozen years.12 Winnie’s Manjama Health Center opened in April 1961; she held clinics in the front of the building and delivered babies in the back.1 By 1967 she was secretary of the church’s Medical Board, responsible for all of its medical work in the country.1

Four of their five children were born in Sierra Leone, and all five grew up there. When they left on June 22, 1968, Winnie wrote simply: “Sierra Leone was home to us for seventeen years.”1

What Lester planted

His own account, written late in life, was titled A Life in Tropical Agroforestry. These are the projects he described from his Sierra Leone years.2

  • Ox-plowingHe bought a pair of oxen from a passing herder, whittled a yoke, and “taught the team to respond to gee and haw in the best Maine fashion.” The chief and his council were not impressed.
  • Cover cropsTo break the cycle of slash-and-burn forest fallow, which tied up land for years, he found a cover crop that kept soil fertile without it.
  • Rhode Island RedsHatching eggs of a larger breed, brooded by local bantam hens — “perfect incubators,” who even taught the chicks to be wary of hawks.
  • WaterHand-dug wells for safe drinking water, and a gravity-feed water system laid out with the chiefdom council.
  • Reading“One of our most rewarding projects was starting an adult literacy program.”
  • Swamp riceInland-valley rice that yielded triple the harvest of rain-fed upland rice — and a simple rat-proof granary to store it.
  • TilapiaFish collected with a net borrowed from the village women, and a pond that thrived. Privately owned ponds, he learned, were better managed than communal ones.
  • Teak“I had to write to the Yale Forestry School librarian to learn how to germinate the teak seed!”
  • Goats’ milkNubian goats, sent through Heifer Project International, for children short of protein. Winnie raised a few orphaned babies on the milk.1
  • Youth clubsStudent apprentices carried the methods home to their villages; the youth agricultural clubs became so popular that village elders insisted on joining.

v

A birth center in the Skagit

Louisiana · Mount Vernon, Washington — 1968 to 1979

Back in the United States, Lester joined the LSU extension service in Louisiana, then earned a Doctor of Education at LSU in 1971 with a study of how well forestry knowledge translated into forest practice in two Louisiana parishes.15 Winnie, a veteran of thousands of deliveries, went back to school too, completing a pediatric residency in Baton Rouge.1

In 1971 they moved to Mount Vernon, Washington, where Lester became Washington State University’s county agent for 4-H, education and forestry in Skagit and Island counties.37

Winnie began attending home births, and in January 1975 she and nurse practitioner Beth Marvin opened the Mount Vernon Birth Center in an old house near Skagit Valley Hospital.1 Within a year or two, nearly two hundred babies had been born there.1 Not every doctor approved. The State Medical Disciplinary Board held a hearing on her competency; that day the hospital waiting room was crowded with families ready to testify on her behalf. One of the doctors involved later wrote to her: “I wanted you to understand how much I think of you as a caring, warm, Christian physician.”1

“Her compassionate approach to birthing,” a Skagit Valley tribute later concluded, “revolutionized the whole birth industry in Skagit County.”4

vi

Sudan & Pakistan

Juba · Khartoum · Tarbela · El Obeid — 1979 to 1989

In October 1979, with the children grown, they were off again. Lester joined Development Alternatives Inc., a USAID contractor, “to work in agroforestry in Southern Sudan.” His team took over two small agriculture and forestry colleges battered by years of civil war, introducing ox-plowing, vegetable and poultry classes, and improved sorghum.21 He went on to become the firm’s head of mission for Sudan, in Khartoum.3

Winnie volunteered at Juba’s hospital and midwifery school, and with a grant from the Netherlands Embassy set out to build three birthing houses for the city. The first was at Kator.16

From 1984 to 1987 they lived at Tarbela, Pakistan, where Lester worked on a project to replace the opium poppy in the hills: dairy cattle, high-altitude poultry, vegetables, silkworms and bees, and a tree nursery that eventually produced up to a million seedlings a year to fight erosion. He was candid about its limits. One villager told him what three years without poppy had cost: “And now,” he said, “We are just poor.”2 Winnie trained a medical team for Afghan refugees.1

In 1989 came one more posting, to El Obeid in northern Sudan, where sand dunes were creeping south over villages. Lester’s nursery raised drought-resistant seedlings, and an “adopt a tree” program had families hand-water them from local oases.2

vii

They never really retired

Mount Vernon and the world — 1991 to 2019

“We retired in 1991,” Lester wrote. “Soon after that I was invited to join a Washington volunteer group to help agroforestry projects in various tropical countries.”2 The short assignments took him to Ukraine, Zimbabwe, Mauritania, Bolivia, Brazil, Indonesia, the Philippines, South Africa and Haiti.3 “In my opinion,” he added, “one of America’s best exports is the Peace Corps.”2

At home he skied, built trails, tutored and swung hammers for Habitat for Humanity, and kept a bird list of the Skagit’s wintering swans, snow geese and raptors.312 He had learned French, Spanish, Latin and German in high school, then Kono, Mende and Krio in Sierra Leone, and later studied Russian.3

Winnie, who had spent her career at the beginning of life, became a hospice volunteer. “I had so enjoyed being a part of the beginning of life,” she said. “Now I wanted to experience being a part of the end of life.”1 She helped found Skagit Adult Day Care; when its expanded building was dedicated in 2005, it was named The Bradford House.1

viii

Letters home

Ten excerpts · London to El Obeid — 1951 to 1989

For nearly forty years Winnie wrote letters home addressed to “Dear Friends,” and Lester took a turn whenever there were snakes, goats or storms to report. Her memoir reproduces dozens of them. These are short excerpts, in their own words, with the names of family left out.1

London, EnglandMay 28, 1951

Studying tropical medicine, a few weeks before sailing for Africa:

“I know London like a book. In fact, that’s the only way I know it — by a book named Geographers’ A to Z Atlas of London. If I get caught without my map (and the various other helps such as bus maps, underground maps) and get into a section of London I haven’t traveled more than three times before, I am lost.”

“The choir is joining with other choirs throughout London and surrounding villages to form a mass choir. It’s called the Free Church Choir Union, with 1,500 voices to sing for the Festival of Britain. … Sir Adrian Boult, who is supposed to be one of the best conductors in Britain, is going to conduct the final concert in June.”

— Winnie1

Rotifunk, Sierra LeoneDecember 1952

Two weeks to plan a wedding, with a church woman “of action” to help:

“Let me tell you the things we had to buy to make the food for the reception. Three bushels of rice, 45 pounds of beef, 30 pounds of pork, 11 chickens, 20 pounds of sugar, 10 pounds of flour, 90 bottles of cola, 10 pounds of onions and 10 pounds of potatoes, in addition to miscellaneous things like salt, pepper, bananas, etc.”

“One handful of rice was thrown, because that’s too precious a commodity in this country to waste by throwing.”

— Winnie1

Rotifunk, Sierra LeoneMarch 1953

Returning from their honeymoon by river launch, in a storm, with the luggage already washed overboard:

“Everyone was frightened, Winnie had three children clinging to her and crying, while several women were hanging on to me, wailing and praying in several languages. The crew was likewise confused and the pilot was issuing conflicting orders and trying to please everyone who offered frantic advice. After much palaver, the issue was settled by fate, since some of the crew got the anchor chain caught in the propeller, disabling the boat completely.”

On the survey tour that followed, choosing a village to work in: “After meeting with the chief of a village, they would tell us how pleased they would be if we were to choose their village to work in, and they would give us a house and land (some even offered to give us a boy and a girl to raise!).”

— Lester1

Kayima, Sierra LeoneNovember 1953

Their first home: a former shop, lent by the chief.

“We never lack for company. There is always someone sitting on our front veranda looking at the ‘cinema’, as they call our View-Master, or just looking to see what we are doing.”

“When we first moved in, there was no kitchen, so we had to set the stove up on the front veranda and cook there. You can imagine the interest in that procedure, since ours is the only stove in all of Kayima.”

— Winnie1

Kayima, Sierra LeoneFebruary 1955

“An amusing event of last week, though potentially alarming, was that our large old rooster was carried off by a wildcat. Fortunately for the cock, he was carried away by his tail instead of his head. The cat chewed his tail off and the fowl escaped and ran away. … We ate the wildcat; the rooster is still running around looking absolutely ridiculous and only half-sized, without his tail.”

— Winnie1

Manjama, Sierra LeoneJuly 1956

Back from furlough, with a dozen Nubian milk goats sent ahead by Heifer Project:

“We found we’d not be sailing with the goats, but we saw them off, and were appreciative of all the effort of others that their sailing represented. We made stops in Portland, Maine … and in Casablanca, Morocco, so the goats beat us to Africa.”

“In the period of adjustment to local conditions, four goats have died. The remainder of the goats are in good condition. … We have high hopes that the remaining goats will be the start of a flock of milk goats that will someday change the nutritional pattern of our young children.”

— Lester1

Manjama, Sierra LeoneOctober 1965

“The bornin’ business has slowed down in the past two months to a mere 15–16 babies a month. In the last three weeks, three sets of twins have been born, a record since I don’t usually have much more than that in a year.”

“I have hired a midwife to help me at the hospital. She has done most of the deliveries, but I still find it difficult to keep hands off.”

— Winnie1

Juba, SudanNovember 1979

Eleven years after leaving Africa, a new posting in Southern Sudan:

“Travel to Juba was hard to arrange since commercial flights are infrequent and unpredictable. … We finally came to Juba by a small, single-engine, five-seat chartered airplane on October 24. So here we are in Southern Sudan, facing an exciting two-and-a-half years of assistance to the department of agriculture in ‘manpower development.’”

“Medical opportunities are unlimited. I have chosen to work through the government hospital in the ‘maternal-child health program.’ There is a midwife school attached to the hospital, so I will be helping with that as well.”

— Lester, continued by Winnie1

Tarbela, PakistanMarch 1985

“I have been holding some training sessions for an Afghan medical team. I have enjoyed getting to know them. Most of them had their medical training (as doctors, nurse midwives, lab technicians, etc.) in Afghanistan before having to leave there. They sometimes have difficulty making the transition to practice largely preventative medicine in the refugee camp situations.”

— Winnie1

El Obeid, SudanMarch 1989

Her first week in the last posting, at sixty-six:

“Our neighbors have five daughters and an infant son. The girls came to call on me my second day here (and each day since), jabbering away in Arabic, and then took me over to meet their mother. They have brought us some kind of food every day.”

“We are settling in fast, learning to think again in pounds and piastres, saying ‘a salaam aleikam’ for hello, eating breakfast in mid-morning, hearing the Moslem calls to prayer, etc. … I don’t anticipate being bored; this is an interesting town.”

— Winnie1

Nurses for Bumpeh Chiefdom

The work continues — 2019 and beyond

Lester died peacefully at home in Mount Vernon on March 3, 2019, after two years of Parkinson’s disease. He was ninety-three.3 Winnie died at home on July 19, 2020, at ninety-seven.613

Memorial gifts went to the Sherbro Foundation, which works in Bumpeh Chiefdom — where Winnie began her career in Rotifunk. In 2020 the foundation dedicated its community health nursing scholarships to her. Each, about $1,100 a year, covers tuition, practicals, supplies, food and transport for a three-year nursing program. The first recipients in her name were Fatmata Sesay, Umu Bangura and Safiatu Bendu.6

It is hard to imagine a better memorial: young women from Rotifunk’s own chiefdom, training to care for its mothers and children.
